The Payload Argument

A 15 MW wind turbine uses about one-thirteenth of the MPSS deck payload rating. That single ratio decides how this platform should be sold — and it is not as a wind foundation.

Prepared forStewart Lang

01The finding

Wind is a light load on a hull built for a much harder job.

The MPSS deck is rated at 30,000 t of payload. The complete rotating machine for a 15 MW turbine — nacelle, rotor and tower — comes to roughly 2,280 t. The turbine is not a challenge for this platform. It is a rounding error against what the deck was designed to carry.

A floating wind foundation is sized to hold up one turbine. The MPSS is sized to hold up an oil and gas production plant. Selling it as the former throws away twelve-thirteenths of what it is.
02The mass picture

Where the buoyancy goes.

At the 27 m operating draft the ring pontoon and the submerged portion of the four columns displace about 56,700 m³ — call it 58,100 t of seawater. That total has to cover the structure itself, the payload on deck, and the ballast that holds the platform at its draft. Working from a structural steel weight of roughly 10,000 t, the budget closes with about 18,100 t left for ballast and margin — which is consistent with the "safety belt of ballast at sea level" the design has always claimed.

The steel figure is on the light side — worth a second look

10,000 t spread over roughly 43,000 m² of shell plating works out to about 233 kg/m², or a 30 mm equivalent plate thickness once stiffening is smeared in. For a box structure at this scale that is credible but lean; comparable semi-submersibles often come out heavier. If the real number is 13–15,000 t, the ballast allowance absorbs it and nothing about the payload argument changes — but the figure should be confirmed before it is quoted externally.

03Loadouts compared

Four payloads still do not fill the deck.

A deepwater production package — separation trains, dry trees, riser support, drilling, quarters — is what consumes a 30,000 t rating. Renewables and adjacent payloads do not come close, individually or together. Even the full four-mission "energy hub" loadout leaves substantial deck capacity unused.

This is why the renewables story is an argument about headroom, not about capability. The platform does not need to prove it can carry a turbine. It needs to show what else it can carry at the same time, on the same mooring, through the same export route.

04The deck

8,100 m², and the load paths run down the columns.

The four corner columns are 15 m square with their centrelines set 7.5 m in from each deck edge. A turbine tower placed on that offset lands dead centre on a column, so its loads run straight down into the ring pontoon rather than into a deck span. The remaining three quadrants take process, storage and compute payloads, with the deck centre kept clear as a roll-on corridor.

05The commercial picture

The market agrees with the engineering.

The same ranking shows up in the lease economics. On the day-rate assumptions already built into the model, oil and gas duty commands the highest rates by a clear margin; wind installation support sits near the bottom of the table. Renewables is not where the revenue density is — it is where the second and third lease is.

Both charts point the same way, so the positioning follows without much argument: lead with the deepwater production host, and let the transition payloads do what they are actually good for — extending the earning life of the asset after the field that paid for it winds down.

One thing left to settle before anything goes out

What the classification societies actually issued. The site now says "independently reviewed" throughout, because nothing is built and therefore nothing is classed or certified. If a formal Approval in Principle or design appraisal document exists, quote it and name it — that reads considerably stronger than the generic wording.

07How to promote it

Sell the host. Let renewables prove the margin.

The earlier promotion plan led with renewables as a headline capability. On the numbers above that is the wrong order. The revised sequence keeps every asset already built — the renders, the day-rate model, the load-path geometry — and simply re-ranks what they are used to argue.

  1. Lead with the deepwater production host It carries the highest day rates, it consumes the full payload rating, and it is the market that is actually commissioning hulls. Nothing about the front door changes.
  2. Use the turbine as evidence of headroom, not as a product line "A 15 MW machine is 7.6% of our deck" is a far stronger sentence than "we do floating wind." The first says the platform is oversized for the hardest thing most people can picture. The second invites a $/MW comparison against WindFloat and Hywind that a general-purpose host will lose.
  3. Sell the second and third lease, not a second market Transition payloads are what keeps the hull earning after its first field. That is a residual-value argument aimed at investors and lenders, not a wind-developer pitch.
  4. Settle the classification wording first It is cheap to fix and it is the kind of thing a technical reader checks early. The payload figures are no longer in question.
  5. Then build the one asset that is missing A single page or sheet that puts Fig 1 and Fig 3 side by side. That comparison is the argument; everything else is supporting material.

What this does not justify is a standalone renewables campaign, a dedicated landing page, or conference spend aimed at floating-wind developers. The earlier plan proposed all three. On these numbers none of them earns its place yet.

Basis and limitations. Geometry is taken from the render series specification (90 × 90 m deck, four 15 m square columns on 75 m centres, continuous 15 m × 9 m ring pontoon, 27 m operating draft). Displacement is computed from that geometry at 1.025 t/m³ and takes no account of appendages, marine growth or free surface. Turbine masses are published figures for the IEA-15-240-RWT reference machine and are not a specific commercial product. Transition payload weights and all day rates are indicative and carry no engineering or commercial warranty.

No wind-specific motion, heel or tower-base fatigue analysis exists for this hull. Nothing in this report should be read as establishing that the MPSS is suitable for a wind loadout — it establishes only that the mass is not the obstacle.
