Floating Wind — Vessel Economics

The vessel delivered cost of floating wind.

Floating offshore wind is a marine-logistics business before it is an energy business. The single biggest lever on installed cost is not the turbine — it is the vessels that pre-lay the moorings, bury the cables, tow the unit out and hook it up. This is a bottom-up study of those costs, built from published day rates and installation schedules and split into pre-turbine-installation and post-turbine-installation marine scope.

The reference unit is a 15 MW turbine on a semi-submersible hull, three catenary mooring lines to three anchor points, ~60–100 km offshore in deep water, with the turbine integrated quayside and towed out complete — the standard floating-wind base case. Figures are delivered marine-spread costs (charter + mobilisation + ~⅓ weather downtime), shown in GBP with USD at ≈ $1.28/£. They are engineering estimates for discussion, not quotes.

The story of floating-wind vessel cost is where the money isn't: because the turbine is craned onto the floater in port and towed out complete, floating wind avoids the offshore turbine installation vessel (WTIV / jack-up) entirely — the most expensive vessel in a bottom-fixed build at $150,000–$250,000/day. The cost migrates instead to anchor-handlers, cable-lay vessels and tugs.

The marine campaign

Delivered in three phases.

  1. 1 · Pre-install
    Anchor-handlers pre-lay the moorings & anchors; the cable-lay vessel installs the dynamic inter-array and export cables. All done before the turbine leaves the quay.
  2. 2 · Tow-out
    The complete, commissioned turbine-on-hull is towed from port to site by a three-tug anchor-handler spread inside a fair-weather window.
  3. 3 · Post-install
    On site: connect to the pre-laid moorings, tension the lines, pull in and terminate the power cable, then commission. O&M vessels take over for the 25–30 year operating life.

Quayside integration is the pivot: it moves the highest-value crane work off the water and into a sheltered, permanently-equipped port.

Scaled up

A 1 GW farm — ≈ 67 × 15 MW.

At array scale the per-turbine spread multiplies out, and the same vessels move continuously between units across a construction window of roughly April–August. Published installation schedules put a two-AHTS campaign at ~127–153 days for ~47 turbines; the marine spread is on charter the whole time.

The one-time marine spread (~£130M) is the pure vessel charter cost. The much larger $1.79bn installation-and-commissioning line includes the hardware being installed (moorings, anchors, cables), developer's insurance, construction project management and contingency — not vessels alone.

Why the numbers look this way

Four cost drivers.

Quayside integration removes the WTIV

Bottom-fixed wind lives or dies on jack-up availability at $150–250k/day. Floating wind cranes the turbine onto the hull in a sheltered port with permanent equipment and tows it out complete. The most expensive vessel in offshore wind simply isn't on the critical path.

Anchor-handlers are the bottleneck

Fewer than ~60 vessels worldwide carry the >250 t bollard pull that floating wind wants — under 4% of the global AHTS fleet, and competing with deepwater oil & gas. Scarcity, not the headline day rate, is the real pre-install cost risk toward 2030.

Weather sets the true rate

Tow-out needs a fair-weather window — Hs ~1–1.5 m and a 72-hour clear forecast to reach site and deploy. With ~⅓ of time lost waiting on weather, the effective day rate is far above the charter rate. Standardised, repeatable operations shrink that penalty.

Tow-to-port changes O&M economics

For major component exchange a floating unit can be disconnected and towed back to port — a 3-AHV spread for ~6–10 days (~£0.8–1.3M/event) — instead of mobilising a jack-up offshore. Day-to-day O&M runs on an SOV (~£30k/day) or CTV (~£2k/day) shared across the array.

Assumptions

  • Reference unit: 15 MW turbine, semi-submersible hull, 3 catenary mooring lines to 3 anchor points, ~60–100 km offshore, deep water, quayside turbine integration.
  • 1 GW array ≈ 67 units of 15 MW. Delivered days include mobilisation and a ~⅓ weather-downtime allowance on the productive window.
  • Currency: GBP shown first, USD at ≈ $1.28/£. Day rates are typical charter/term rates, generally excluding fuel unless noted.
  • Figures are engineering estimates assembled from public sources for discussion — not offers, quotes or project-specific costs.
