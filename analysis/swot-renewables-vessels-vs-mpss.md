# SWOT: Renewable Projects · Renewable-Support Vessels · The MPSS

**Prepared:** August 2026 · **Status:** internal working analysis, not investor-facing copy

Three SWOTs and a comparison, prepared to test one question the site and the memorandum
currently leave open: **how much of the MPSS case should rest on renewables?**

The site (`index.html`), the memorandum (`memo.html`), the day-rate model (`day-rates.js`)
and the render series (`render/mpssdeckloadingscenes.md`) all lean on an energy-transition
story — a 15 MW turbine on the north-west column, CCS on the south-west, BESS on the
south-east, a data centre on the north-east. This document SWOTs that market, SWOTs the
vessel fleet that serves it, SWOTs the MPSS against both, and compares the results.

---

## 0. Scope — what each of the three things is

| Unit of analysis | What it covers here |
|---|---|
| **A. Renewable projects** | Offshore wind (fixed-bottom and floating), plus the adjacent offshore transition missions the repo already sells against: CCS, offshore green hydrogen, offshore power/BESS, offshore data centres. The *asset owner / developer* position. |
| **B. Renewable-support vessels** | WTIVs and offshore heavy-lift, SOV/CSOVs, CSVs and cable-lay, feeder barges, AHTS/tow spreads for floating wind. The *contractor / tonnage owner* position. |
| **C. The MPSS** | A moored, low-motion, 90 × 90 m box semi-submersible host: 30,000 t deck payload, 8,100 m² deck, single 27 m operating draft, ~14-month hull, LR/ABS/DNV reviewed, leased on multi-year day rate. The *infrastructure landlord* position. |

These are three different businesses, not three versions of one. That distinction turns out
to drive the whole comparison.

---

## A. SWOT — Renewable projects (offshore wind and adjacent transition assets)

### Strengths
- **Long-dated, contracted revenue.** CfDs, PPAs and offtake contracts run 15–20 years — far
  longer than any oil-and-gas charter, and largely insulated from the commodity price.
- **Policy-manufactured demand.** Where the mandate holds, demand is legislated rather than
  discovered — national net-zero targets, seabed leasing rounds, CCS storage licences.
- **Investment-grade counterparties.** Utilities and oil majors (Ørsted, Equinor, EnBW, RWE,
  bp) sit on the other side of the contract, not marginal E&P independents.
- **Genuine technical adjacency for a low-motion host.** Floating wind, offshore hydrogen,
  offshore CCS injection and offshore data centres all need exactly what the MPSS sells:
  deck area, payload, station-keeping and low heave.

### Weaknesses
- **The economics do not carry a $600 M host.** A semi-submersible floating-wind substructure
  runs roughly **€430–485 k per MW installed** — about €15 M for a 12 MW unit. One 15 MW
  machine on an MPSS corner column is ~€6–7 M of "substructure value" sitting on a platform
  the memo prices at $600 M. As a *wind generator*, the MPSS is off by two orders of magnitude.
- **Power is the lowest-value cargo per square metre of deck.** Wind sells electrons at a
  strike price; oil sells barrels. That gap shows up directly in the repo's own day rates
  (see §D).
- **Chronic schedule slip.** Consenting, grid connection, port capacity and turbine supply
  each independently delay first power; the compounded schedule is the sector's defining risk.
- **Fabrication and port bottlenecks are the real constraint** on floating wind — not hull
  design. Quayside area and load-out capacity gate the pipeline, and the MPSS does not
  relieve that; it competes for the same quay.

### Opportunities
- **Floating wind is still pre-industrial** — no dominant substructure design, no serial
  production line. Whoever industrialises the floater wins a market that does not yet have
  an incumbent. The MPSS's serial-build thesis is aimed at precisely this gap.
- **The adjacent missions are less crowded than wind.** Offshore CCS, offshore hydrogen and
  offshore data centres have no standard host at all — that is a blanker sheet than wind.
- **Offshore data centres are the outlier**: power-hungry, latency-tolerant if sited well,
  and backed by counterparties whose cost of capital and willingness to pay for capacity
  dwarf anything in the wind supply chain. Of the repo's eight tenant presets, it is the
  only renewable-adjacent one that clears the hurdle rate.
- **A recovery is being called for 2026** after two brutal years — improving conditions on
  several fronts, with AR7 in the UK adding secured floating offtake.

### Threats
- **A live, ongoing wave of cancellations.** EnBW halted its 3 GW Mona and Morgan projects in
  the UK Irish Sea; the 58.4 MW Blyth 2 floating project was cancelled in January 2026; in
  February 2026 the US government suspended leases on five large projects including Equinor's
  Empire Wind. This is not historic — it is the current operating environment.
- **The sector has switched from growth-at-any-cost to strict capital discipline.** Every
  developer is now cutting scope, not adding a novel, unproven host to the critical path.
- **A thin floating pipeline with murky timelines.** The 2026 floating pipeline gained only
  ~193 MW of secured offtake. That is a fraction of one MPSS's worth of capital.
- **Political reversibility.** Unlike an oil field, the demand is a policy artefact and can be
  withdrawn by an election — as the US lease suspensions demonstrate.

## B. SWOT — Renewable-support vessels (WTIV, SOV/CSOV, CSV, feeder, tow)

### Strengths
- **Proven, liquid business model.** Day-rate chartering of tonnage is a century-old,
  bankable structure with an established owner base, standard charterparties and deep
  second-hand markets.
- **Very high rates at the top of the fleet.** High-spec WTIVs are valued around
  **$500 k/day**, with wait-on-weather for a full spread running **$18–22 k/hour** in 2026.
- **Mobility is the core asset.** A vessel moves to demand. Where the North Sea softens, the
  hull sails to Taiwan or the US East Coast — the single most valuable structural feature
  the MPSS does not have.
- **O&M demand is structural, not cyclical.** Every turbine installed becomes 20–25 years of
  SOV demand regardless of whether new FIDs are taken.

### Weaknesses
- **Utilisation risk sits with the owner.** Idle steel earns nothing, and the spread between
  a chartered and an idle year is the whole return.
- **Long-charter SOVs earn thin returns.** Guaranteed-utilisation charters trade at
  comparatively low day rates and **often yield single-digit IRRs** — the safety is priced in.
- **Turbine upscaling obsoletes tonnage.** A jack-up specified for 8 MW machines cannot install
  15 MW; deck strength, leg length and crane height all date the asset. Modern SOVs run
  **€45–60 k/day**, ~30% above 2020, but that is a rate for the *current* generation only.
- **Capital-intensive with no alternative use.** A WTIV outside offshore wind is a stranded
  asset with a very short buyer list.

### Opportunities
- **Europe tightens later in the decade** — supply of ≥15 MW-capable WTIVs is forecast tight
  from 2032, and from 2030 in other markets. Patient, well-timed tonnage gets paid.
- **Floating wind changes the vessel mix**, shifting work from jack-ups toward tow, mooring
  and offshore-assembly spreads — a re-shuffle that opens the door for new asset types.
- **Consolidation is under way** — fleet sales signal a maturing sector where scale owners buy
  distressed tonnage cheaply.

### Threats
- **A structural oversupply, now arriving.** More than **60 WTIVs and heavy-lift vessels are
  scheduled for delivery 2026–2030**; ≥15 MW-capable supply outside China goes from none in
  2020 to **over 25 by 2028**. EAPAC and North America are forecast oversupplied across the
  forecast horizon.
- **The same story in the service fleet**: ~**60 SOV/CSOV deliveries by end-2026**, against 40
  in 2025, into a market widely forecast oversupplied 2025–2030 with volatile day rates.
- **Rate collapse is the base case, not the tail case** — the 2022–24 record rates were a
  shortage artefact, and the shortage is being fixed by the orderbook.
- **Project cancellations hit the vessel market twice**: less installation work now, and fewer
  turbines to service for the next 25 years.

## C. SWOT — The MPSS

### Strengths
- **The engineering risk is genuinely behind it.** Four decades of work, 1:100 tank testing,
  doctoral research at Glasgow and Newcastle, peer-reviewed publication, and classification
  review by Lloyd's Register, ABS and DNV. Very few offshore concepts can say this.
- **The build sequence is the real differentiator.** Deck box outfitted at quay level, hull
  floated under it last (`render/mpssdeckloadingscenes.md`, Scenes 2–6). This removes the
  offshore/afloat lift and roughly 25–30 m of hook height — it does not remove the crane, but
  it de-risks the most expensive hour in the schedule.
- **Load path integrity.** The corner-column geometry — tower centreline 7.5 m inboard,
  landing dead centre on a 15 m square column — puts point loads straight into the ring
  pontoon rather than a deck span. This is a defensible technical claim, and the render
  series already makes it visually.
- **Low motion by construction.** A deep ring pontoon at 27 m sees ~40% of surface wave
  height; tested to a 37.5 m survival wave with no water on deck and no change of draft.
- **Standardisation is the actual product.** Serial hulls, one classed design, topside-swapped
  per mission — leaseable inventory available *before* FID. Nobody in floating wind has
  industrialised anything comparable.
- **Real residual value.** A classed, redeployable steel hull is hard collateral in a way a
  project SPV never is.

### Weaknesses
- **The renewable tenants cannot pay the required rate.** On the repo's own inputs, the
  ~15% unlevered yield in `memo.html` is achievable on **one** preset: oil production. Every
  renewable and transition tenant lands between 3.9% and 9.2% (§D).
- **A naming error that will be caught in diligence.** `day-rates.js:15` sells
  *"Offshore wind turbine install"* at $180 k/day for 2 years. The MPSS is a moored host
  with no heavy-lift crane and no transit mode — **it is not an installation vessel**, and
  cannot compete with a $500 k/day WTIV for that scope. The turbine in the renders stands
  *on* the platform as a generator; it is not being installed *by* it. Two different
  businesses are currently sharing one line item.
- **Immobile by design.** The mobility that lets a WTIV chase demand across basins is exactly
  what a moored host gives up. Redeployment is a de-mob/tow/re-moor campaign, not a voyage.
- **Not yet built.** Classed and reviewed is not the same as delivered. First-of-class risk,
  yard slot risk and the ~14-month hull claim are all unproven in steel.
- **Single-asset concentration.** One $600 M hull, one tenant at a time. A vessel owner
  diversifies across a fleet; the founding round does not.
- **Oversized for the wind mission it depicts.** A 90 × 90 m deck carrying one 15 MW machine
  is the wrong product; the same deck carrying BESS + CCS + data centre + power conversion
  is a different and much better one. The render series shows both and does not choose.

### Opportunities
- **Reposition wind from generator to grid-and-services hub.** The value is not one turbine on
  a corner column — it is the **substation, BESS, conversion, hydrogen and accommodation
  functions** for a whole floating array, on one classed platform that also survives a 37.5 m
  wave. That is a scope no floater competes for and no vessel can hold station for.
- **Offshore data centre is the strongest transition tenant in the repo's own model** — a
  10-year term at 95% utilisation is the only non-hydrocarbon preset that clears the assumed
  8% debt cost with room to spare.
- **CCS and hydrogen sell term, not rate.** 12-year and 10-year charters at 8.2–8.3%
  unlevered are the closest renewable analogue to infrastructure yield — attractive to a
  different investor than the memo currently addresses.
- **Counter-cyclical yard pricing.** Cancellations free yard slots and soften steel and
  fabrication pricing. The best time to order serial hulls is a bad market.
- **The vessel oversupply is an argument *for* the MPSS**, not against it: a permanently
  moored host substitutes for chartered tonnage over a 10–20 year life, converting a volatile
  day-rate exposure into a fixed one.

### Threats
- **The hydrocarbon core carries the returns, and the renewable story carries the marketing.**
  Any investor who runs the day-rate model finds this in ten minutes. It should be stated
  first, by the memo, rather than discovered.
- **Renewables counterparties are cutting, not committing.** Under strict capital discipline,
  no developer adds a first-of-class host to the critical path.
- **The comparison the market will actually make** is against known things — a WindFloat-class
  floater, a chartered CSOV, an FPSO conversion — not against the "conventional bespoke FPU"
  the site compares to. The comparison table in `index.html` answers a question nobody in
  renewables is asking.
- **Illustrative figures marked as such can still anchor badly.** `$600 M`, `~15%`, `2x MOIC`
  and `60% residual` are placeholders in `invest.js` / `memo.html`, but they read as claims.
- **First-mover risk is unpriced.** Serial production requires a second and third order that
  do not exist until the first hull earns.

## D. The comparison

### D.1 The repo's own model, run tenant by tenant

Every preset in `day-rates.js`, taken at face value, against the `invest.js` deal
($600 M asset, 55% debt at 8% → $26.4 M/yr interest, $270 M equity):

| Tenant (as defined in `day-rates.js`) | Term | Net $M/yr | Unlevered yield | Equity CF $M/yr | Cash-on-equity |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Oil production host (FPSO) | 8 y | 94.0 | **15.7%** | 67.6 | **25.0%** |
| Drilling support | 3 y | 72.9 | 12.2% | 46.5 | 17.2% |
| Offshore data center | 10 y | 55.5 | 9.2% | 29.1 | 10.8% |
| Green hydrogen | 10 y | 49.8 | 8.3% | 23.4 | 8.7% |
| CCS / carbon storage | 12 y | 49.3 | 8.2% | 22.9 | 8.5% |
| Power generation | 10 y | 37.8 | 6.3% | 11.4 | 4.2% |
| Offshore wind turbine install | 2 y | 36.5 | 6.1% | 10.1 | 3.7% |
| Accommodation / flotel | 2 y | 23.4 | 3.9% | −3.0 | **−1.1%** |

Three things fall out of this table:

1. **The memo's headline is the oil case.** `invest.js` defaults to `netCharter: 94000000` —
   which is, to the dollar, the oil-production preset at 92% utilisation. The "~15%
   illustrative unlevered net yield" in `memo.html` §06 is not a blended or renewable figure.
2. **Leverage only works above 8%.** At the assumed debt cost, gearing is accretive for oil,
   drilling, data centre, hydrogen and (marginally) CCS. For power generation, wind install
   and accommodation, the debt eats the spread — and the flotel case goes cash-negative.
3. **Term and rate trade against each other in the right direction.** CCS at 12 years and
   hydrogen at 10 pay less per day but far more predictably than 2-year wind or flotel work.
   For an infrastructure buyer, an 8.2% yield on a 12-year contract is a *better* asset than
   12.2% on a 3-year one — which the memo does not currently argue.

### D.2 Structural comparison

| Dimension | Renewable projects | Support vessels | MPSS |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Position in the chain** | Asset owner / developer | Contractor, tonnage supplier | Infrastructure landlord |
| **Revenue** | Power/offtake price × output | Day rate × utilisation | Day rate × utilisation |
| **Contract length** | 15–20 y (CfD/PPA) | 2–10 y, spot to term | 2–12 y by mission |
| **Who holds utilisation risk** | Developer (wind resource) | **Owner** | **Owner** |
| **Mobility** | Fixed for life | **High — chases demand** | Low; moored, tow to redeploy |
| **Current supply balance** | Demand shrinking (cancellations) | **Oversupplied 2025–2030** | No supply — none built |
| **Rate direction** | Strike prices under pressure | Softening from 2022–24 peak | Unproven |
| **Capital per unit** | €430–485 k/MW substructure | ~$300–500 M+ high-spec WTIV | $600 M/host (illustrative) |
| **Obsolescence** | 25–30 y asset life | **High — turbine upscaling** | Low — topsides swap, hull stays |
| **Residual value** | Site-bound, decom liability | Liquid but cyclical | Hard steel, redeployable (60% assumed) |
| **Counterparty** | Government / utility | Developer / EPC | Operator / utility / hyperscaler |
| **Main risk** | Policy and consenting | Utilisation and rate collapse | **First-of-class and single-asset** |
| **Best return in repo's model** | 6.1–9.2% unlevered | Single-digit IRR on term charters | 15.7% unlevered — on oil |

### D.3 What the comparison actually says

**The MPSS is structurally closer to a vessel than to a renewable project — and it inherits
the vessel model's weakness (owner-held utilisation risk) without its strength (mobility).**
It compensates with two things a vessel does not have: **topside reconfigurability**, which
defeats the turbine-upscaling obsolescence that dates a WTIV, and **a 27 m fixed draft with a
37.5 m survival wave**, which lets it hold station through weather that sends a CSOV to port.
Those two features are the honest core of the MPSS's advantage over tonnage, and the site
currently buries them under a five-S philosophy pitch.

**Against renewable projects, the MPSS is not a competitor and should stop implying it is.**
It cannot compete with a €15 M semi-submersible floater as a 15 MW generator platform, and it
cannot compete with a $500 k/day WTIV as an installer. What it can do — and what no floater or
vessel does — is hold a **large, powered, low-motion, permanently-manned deck on station for
20 years**, which is the natural home for an array substation, BESS, hydrogen production, CO₂
compression and injection, a data centre, or all of them at once. That is the argument the
render series' Scene 5 already makes visually, and the copy does not make commercially.

**The timing argument cuts both ways.** The vessel market's oversupply and the project
market's cancellations both make renewables a bad *first* tenant — nobody is committing capital
to a first-of-class host in this environment. The same conditions make it an excellent
*second* tenant: a hull ordered into a soft yard market and paid for by an oil-production
charter is positioned to take the transition work when floating wind industrialises later
in the decade, exactly as European ≥15 MW vessel supply tightens from 2030–32.

## E. Recommendations that follow from the comparison

1. **Lead the memorandum with the hydrocarbon case and name it as such.** The ~15% figure is
   the oil-production preset. Stating that up front converts a diligence liability into a
   credibility asset; the transition tenants become optionality on top of an oil-underwritten
   return, which is a stronger structure than a blended claim nobody can reproduce.
2. **Fix `day-rates.js:15`.** Rename *"Offshore wind turbine install"* to something the MPSS
   can actually do — *"Floating wind hub — substation, BESS & O&M base"* — and re-rate it on
   a 10-year term. The current line invites the question "where is your crane?"
3. **Promote the offshore data centre.** It is the best-yielding non-hydrocarbon tenant in the
   repo's own model (9.2% unlevered, 10.8% on equity, 10-year term, 95% utilisation) and it
   faces a counterparty class with a genuinely different willingness to pay.
4. **Sell CCS and hydrogen on term, not rate.** 12 and 10 years of contracted cash flow at
   ~8% unlevered is an infrastructure product for an infrastructure buyer — a different and
   larger pool of capital than the family offices `invest.html` currently targets.
5. **Add the comparison the market will actually make.** The hull table in `index.html`
   benchmarks against a conventional twin-pontoon FPU. Add a second table benchmarking against
   a floating-wind semi and a chartered CSOV — the MPSS wins on deck, payload, station-keeping
   and mission life, and that is a fight worth picking publicly.
6. **Use the renders' Scene 8 as the wind story, not Scene 4.** Four identical hulls under four
   loadouts is the standardisation argument. A single turbine being erected on a corner column
   is a $600 M platform doing a €15 M job, and a wind specialist will say so.
