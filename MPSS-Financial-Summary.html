01
The value created

Avoided capital and time — the two things that matter

Avoided CAPEX

A conventional deepwater production floater — FPSO, production semi, spar or TLP — commits hundreds of millions to well over a billion dollars of capital, tied up in a single field for its life. MILC aims to deliver equivalent hull capability from a standardized asset costing a fraction of that, provided on a lease. The customer's benefit is not a cheaper hull — it is not having to commit the capital at all, and being free to deploy it into drilling, reserves, or other projects that earn far more than a steel platform.

Time to first production

Bespoke floaters take years from concept selection through engineering, fabrication and installation. A standardized hull, available when needed, has the potential to compress that schedule substantially.

For a field flowing 150,000–200,000 bbl/day, every month of earlier start-up brings forward roughly $300M+ of gross production. Time creates value. Steel does not.

Illustrative: ~175,000 bbl/day × ~$60/bbl × 30 days ≈ $315M of gross production value per month advanced — a customer-side, order-of-magnitude figure, not MILC revenue, and dependent on field, price and schedule.

The full customer proposition

  • Lower upfront capital commitment
  • Earlier production and cash flow
  • Reduced financing burden and balance-sheet exposure
  • Standardized, proven infrastructure — with potential fleet availability
  • Reduced end-of-life ownership and decommissioning liability
  • Capital freed to redeploy where it earns more
02
The model

Why leasing — customers buy production, not steel

Owning the physical asset is rarely the point. Airlines don't need to own aircraft; they need seats flying. Operators don't need to own a floater; they need production capacity on station. Leasing lets them pay for the capability and keep their capital working.

MILC owns the infrastructure. Customers buy production.

This is not a novel financial experiment — it is the established model of the most capital-intensive industries on earth:

  • Aircraft leasing — roughly half the world's commercial fleet is leased, not owned.
  • FPSO financing — offshore operators already lease floating production on long-term day-rate charters (the direct precedent for MILC's structure).
  • Rail leasing — locomotives and rolling stock, owned by lessors, operated by railroads.
  • Commercial shipping — bareboat and time charters underpin global trade.

05
The obvious question

Why hasn't this been done already?

Because today's semi-submersibles were never designed for it. They evolved from drilling rigs — bespoke, bracing-heavy structures optimized for a drilling programme, not for repeatable production or serial fabrication. The MPSS was conceived from the outset as a standardized production platform, engineered around a different set of priorities:

  • Simplicity — closed flat-panel box-girder construction, no delicate bracing
  • Fabrication efficiency — ~18 man-hours/tonne vs. 150–180 for a conventional semi
  • Low motion — natural periods (~24 s heave, ~42 s pitch/roll) outside the swell-resonance band
  • Repeatability — standardized blocks built for a production run, not a one-off
  • Long service life — 50–70-year hull, designed to be re-used
06
The business

An infrastructure platform, not a construction company

MILC is not in the business of selling hulls. It owns long-life assets and monetizes them repeatedly over decades. Each hull moves through a lifecycle:

Build→ Lease→ Return→ Refurbish→ Re-lease ↺

End-of-life becomes a beginning

Conventional offshore projects end in expensive decommissioning. Under MILC's model the operator returns the hull rather than scrapping it; MILC refurbishes and redeploys it where commercially appropriate. Field-specific topsides may or may not carry over — that depends on engineering, remaining life and the next project — but the hull itself retains value. The customer sheds a decommissioning liability; MILC keeps a long-life asset.

Defensibility

The moat, the yard, and long-term value

Why it's hard to copy

  • 35 years of validated, class-reviewed design and accumulated know-how, held under disclosure control
  • A fabrication method (standardized flat-panel blocks) that few have engineered for production hulls
  • A demand-aggregating leasing model that makes a serial run bankable — hard to assemble from a standing start
  • First-mover fleet, yard relationship and class lineage

Why Hanwha Ocean

  • One of the world's largest, most capable shipbuilders
  • Modular, high-automation serial fabrication capacity
  • Access to export-credit (ECA) financing that lowers cost of capital
  • Confidence in repeatable production at fleet scale

What "50–70-year hull life" actually means

1st lease→ 2nd lease→ 3rd lease→ 4th lease→ Residual asset

A hull amortized over ~20 years is substantially deleveraged by the end of its first 10–15-year lease, then re-let again and again. Terminal value is not scrap — it is a class-maintained infrastructure asset with decades of remaining life. Ownership, not construction, is where the long-term value sits.

The investment thesis

In one paragraph

MILC is not attempting to build another offshore platform. It is standardizing floating offshore infrastructure and applying a proven long-term leasing model to an industry that has historically relied on bespoke capital projects. If successful, customers reduce capital commitments, accelerate project delivery, and avoid long-term asset ownership — while MILC builds a fleet of long-life infrastructure assets that can be leased repeatedly over multiple decades. The hull is simply the mechanism; the product is time and capital efficiency.
